The discovery of storage costs had changed everything. Now traders were beginning to think strategically about which goods to accept for indirect trading. But this new approach required something that had never been common in their society: **speculation**.

## The Speculation Challenge

Kael arrived at the next festival with a new mindset. He was no longer just looking for direct trades—he was looking for opportunities to accept goods he didn't immediately need, but that might help him get what he wanted later. This required a leap of faith that many traders found difficult.

"Kael," said Tira, approaching him with a small obsidian tool, "I know you don't need this, but it's light, durable, and easy to carry. Would you take it for your barley? You could probably trade it for tools later."

Kael examined the obsidian tool. It was indeed well-crafted and portable, but he had never used obsidian before. "How do I know it's valuable?" he asked. "What if no one wants it when I try to trade it?"

## The Information Problem

This was the core challenge of speculation: **How do you know what goods will be valuable in the future?** The traders had no way to predict what others would want, and they had limited information about what goods were in demand.

Jorek joined the conversation. "I've been thinking about this too. I need grain and tools, but I'm willing to accept other goods if I think they'll help me get what I need. But how do I decide what to accept?"

Tira nodded. "And how do I know what to offer? I don't want to give away my best goods for something that might be worthless."

## The Learning Process

As the festival continued, the traders began to develop strategies for dealing with this information problem. They started to observe patterns in what goods were being traded and what goods were in demand.

"I've noticed that obsidian tools are always popular," Kael observed. "People seem to want them for cutting and carving. Maybe I should be more willing to accept them."

Tira agreed. "And I've seen that small copper ornaments are being traded more and more. People are starting to prefer them for indirect trading."

Jorek looked thoughtful. "But what about my wool? It's useful, but it's expensive to store. Will people still want it for indirect trading?"

## The Social Learning Effect

The traders began to learn from each other's behavior. When they saw someone successfully trade a particular good, they became more likely to accept that good themselves. This created a feedback loop that would prove crucial for the emergence of money.

"Did you see how easily Kael traded that obsidian tool?" Tira asked Jorek. "Maybe I should start accepting obsidian tools too. They seem to be in high demand."

Jorek nodded. "And I noticed that people are more willing to accept small copper ornaments than heavy tools. Maybe I should focus on making smaller, more portable goods."

## The Emergence of Conventions

As the festival progressed, certain patterns began to emerge. Some goods were becoming more popular for indirect trading than others, and traders were starting to develop shared expectations about what goods were "good for trading."

"I'm starting to think of obsidian tools as 'trading goods,'" Kael said. "I don't need them myself, but I know I can probably trade them for what I do need."

Tira agreed. "And small copper ornaments are becoming 'trading goods' too. People seem to prefer them over heavy tools for indirect trading."

## The Speculation Strategy

The traders began to develop a new trading strategy based on speculation and social learning:

1. **Accept goods you don't need** if they have low storage costs
2. **Observe what goods are popular** for indirect trading
3. **Learn from successful trades** and adjust your strategy
4. **Develop preferences** for goods that are commonly accepted

This strategy was risky—it required accepting goods you might not be able to trade later—but it was also potentially very rewarding.

## The Network Effect

As more traders began to use this speculation strategy, a network effect emerged. The more people who accepted a particular good for indirect trading, the more valuable that good became for speculation.

"Now that everyone is accepting obsidian tools," Kael observed, "they're becoming more valuable for indirect trading. The more people who want them, the easier they are to trade."

Tira nodded. "And the same thing is happening with small copper ornaments. They're becoming the preferred 'trading goods' because everyone knows they're easy to trade."

## The Birth of Money

By the end of the festival, something remarkable had happened. The traders had begun to converge on a small set of goods that were preferred for indirect trading. These goods had become more than just commodities—they had become **intermediary goods** that could be used to facilitate any trade.

"I'm starting to think of obsidian tools and small copper ornaments as 'universal goods,'" Kael said. "I can trade them for anything I need, and I know others will accept them."

Tira agreed. "It's like they've become a common language for trade. Everyone knows what they're worth and how to use them."

## The Speculation Revolution

The speculation strategy had revolutionized trading. By accepting goods they didn't immediately need, traders had created a system of indirect exchange that was more efficient than direct barter. But more importantly, they had created the conditions for the emergence of money.

The key insight was that **speculation and social learning could create shared expectations about which goods were suitable for indirect trading**. These shared expectations would eventually lead to the emergence of a common medium of exchange.

## The Path Forward

As the traders prepared to leave the festival, they realized that they were on the verge of something revolutionary. The speculation strategy was working, but it was still inefficient. There were too many different "trading goods," and traders had to guess which ones would be accepted.

*What if*, Kael wondered, *what if everyone could agree on just one or two goods for indirect trading? What if we could create a universal standard that everyone would accept?*

The answer to this question would change the course of economic history.
