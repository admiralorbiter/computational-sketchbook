The success of the first indirect trades had spread quickly through the settlements. But as more traders began experimenting with this new approach, they discovered a hidden cost that would shape the future of trade: **storage costs**.

## The Problem of Holding Goods

Kael had learned his lesson from the previous festival. This time, he arrived with a different strategy—he would accept goods he didn't immediately need, but only if they were easy to store and carry. But as he looked around the trading area, he realized that not all goods were created equal when it came to storage.

Tira approached him with a proposition. "Kael, I have these beautiful copper ornaments I made. They're small, they don't spoil, and they're valuable. Would you take them for your barley? You could easily carry them around and trade them later."

Kael examined the ornaments. They were indeed small and well-crafted, but something bothered him. "Tira, these are beautiful, but they're heavy. And if I'm going to carry them around looking for trades, the weight will slow me down."

## The Discovery of Storage Costs

Jorek overheard their conversation and joined them. "I have the opposite problem," he said. "I have wool textiles that are light and easy to carry, but they take up a lot of space. And if they get wet or damaged, they lose their value quickly."

Kael nodded thoughtfully. "So we have three different types of goods: heavy but durable, light but bulky, and... what about grain?"

"Grain is the worst of both worlds," Tira said. "It's heavy, it takes up space, and it spoils quickly. If you don't use it or trade it soon, it becomes worthless."

## The Storage Cost Analysis

As the three traders discussed their experiences, they began to see a pattern. Each good had different costs associated with holding it:

**Barley (Grain):**
- Heavy to carry
- Takes up significant space
- Spoils quickly (high storage cost)
- Must be used or traded within weeks

**Copper Tools and Ornaments:**
- Heavy but compact
- Don't spoil or deteriorate
- Moderate storage cost
- Can be held for months or years

**Wool Textiles:**
- Light but bulky
- Susceptible to damage
- High storage cost due to space and fragility
- Must be protected from elements

## The Trading Implications

Kael realized that this storage cost difference was crucial for indirect trading. "If I'm going to accept goods I don't immediately need," he said, "I want goods that are cheap to store. I don't want to carry around heavy grain or fragile textiles if I can help it."

Tira nodded. "And if I'm going to accept goods speculatively, I want to know that they'll still be valuable when I find someone to trade with. Copper ornaments might be heavy, but they don't lose their value over time."

Jorek looked concerned. "But that means my textiles are at a disadvantage. They're useful, but they're expensive to store. People might not want to accept them for indirect trading."

## The Emergence of Preferences

As the festival continued, Kael noticed that traders were beginning to develop preferences based on storage costs. Goods that were cheap to store—like small copper ornaments or obsidian tools—were becoming more popular for indirect trading.

"Everyone wants my copper ornaments," Tira observed. "But they're not as interested in my heavy tools, even though the tools are more useful."

Kael nodded. "It makes sense. If you're going to hold something between trades, you want it to be easy to carry and store. The ornaments are perfect for that."

## The Storage Cost Hierarchy

By the end of the festival, a clear hierarchy had emerged based on storage costs:

1. **Low Storage Cost**: Small copper ornaments, obsidian tools, precious stones
2. **Medium Storage Cost**: Copper tools, pottery, some textiles
3. **High Storage Cost**: Grain, fresh food, bulky textiles, livestock

This hierarchy was beginning to influence trading decisions in ways that would have profound implications for the future of exchange.

## The Speculation Strategy

Kael began to develop a new trading strategy based on storage costs. "I'll accept low storage cost goods even if I don't need them immediately," he told his fellow traders. "They're cheap to hold, and I can trade them later for what I actually need."

Tira agreed. "And I'll be more selective about what I accept. I don't want to be stuck with heavy or perishable goods that are expensive to store."

Jorek looked worried. "But what does this mean for goods with high storage costs? Will people still want to trade for them?"

## The Beginning of Money

As the traders packed up their goods, Kael had a realization. The storage cost differences were creating a natural selection process. Goods that were cheap to store were becoming more popular for indirect trading, while goods that were expensive to store were becoming less popular.

This was the beginning of a process that would eventually lead to the emergence of money. The goods with the lowest storage costs were becoming the preferred "intermediary goods" for indirect trading.

*What if*, Kael wondered, *what if everyone started to prefer the same low storage cost goods for indirect trading? What if we all agreed that certain goods were better for this purpose than others?*

## The Storage Cost Revolution

The discovery of storage costs had revolutionized trading. It wasn't just about what goods were useful or valuable—it was also about what goods were cheap to store and carry. This simple insight would change the course of economic history.

As Kael walked home, he realized that the storage cost problem was actually a solution in disguise. By making some goods more suitable for indirect trading than others, it was creating the conditions for the emergence of a common medium of exchange.

The question was: which goods would emerge as the preferred intermediaries? And what would happen when everyone started using the same goods for indirect trading?
