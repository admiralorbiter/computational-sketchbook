# Prior Research Context & Scholarly Positioning

This document situates the three Missouri descriptive measurement studies within the empirical literature on advanced secondary coursework, administrative survey indicators, and educational opportunity metrics. 

> [!NOTE]
> **Role of Related Literature**: Related empirical literature provides conceptual and historical context on course availability, school size, and program substitution. It does not by itself establish novelty. The contribution of this sketchbook project is strictly methodological and descriptive: providing an auditable, bounded empirical replication demonstrating how administrative survey definitions and denominator choices alter descriptions of educational opportunity in a single state cohort.

---

## 1. AP vs. Dual Enrollment: Multiple Pathways to Advanced Credit

A common limitation of early college-readiness research was treating the College Board's Advanced Placement (AP) program as an exhaustive proxy for college-level opportunity.

### Verified Prior Studies:
* **Malkus, Nat (American Enterprise Institute, January 2016)**:
  * *The AP Peak: Public Schools Offering Advanced Placement, 2000–12*. Washington, DC: AEI. [Report Link](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/the-ap-peak-public-high-school-ap-offerings-and-participation-by-the-numbers/) | [Direct PDF](https://www.aei.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/The-AP-Peak.pdf)
  * *Pages 6–8, Figure 4 & Table 2*: Analyzed public high school AP offerings by enrollment quartiles and NCES locales (urban, suburban, town, rural). Malkus showed that while national AP offerings expanded through 2008, small and non-suburban schools face severe structural limits in AP provision: in 2012, fewer than 66% of rural high schools offered AP courses compared to 91% of suburban high schools, with offerings tightly correlated with school enrollment scale.
  * Companion Study: Malkus, Nat. (January 2016). *AP at Scale: Public School Students in Advanced Placement, 1990–2013*. Washington, DC: AEI. [Report Link](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/ap-at-scale-public-school-students-in-advanced-placement-1990-2013/) | [Direct PDF](https://www.aei.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/AP-at-Scale.pdf).
* **ACT, Inc. (2015)**:
  * *Using Dual Enrollment to Improve the Educational Outcomes of High School Students*. ACT Policy Brief. Iowa City, IA: ACT. [Report Link](https://www.act.org/content/act/en/research/reports/act-publications/using-dual-enrollment.html)
  * *Pages 1–4*: Examined how dual enrollment serves as an alternative postsecondary credit mechanism, highlighting the growth of dual credit partnerships outside large suburban districts.
* **National Center for Education Statistics (NCES, December 2020)**:
  * *Dual or Concurrent Enrollment in Public Schools in the United States* (NCES 2020-125). U.S. Department of Education. Washington, DC: Institute of Education Sciences. [Report Link](https://nces.ed.gov/pubsearch/pubsinfo.asp?pubid=2020125)
  * *Table 1*: Using data from the 2017–18 National Teacher and Principal Survey (NTPS), reported that 73% of public high schools offered dual enrollment courses, with especially high adoption in rural and town communities where community college articulation agreements serve as the primary vehicle for advanced coursework.
* **Fink, John, Jenkins, Davis, & Yanagiura, Takeshi (CCRC, September 2017)**:
  * *What Happens to Students Who Take the Dual Enrollment Highway?* Community College Research Center, Teachers College, Columbia University. [Report Link](https://ccrc.tc.columbia.edu/publications/what-happens-advanced-honors-students-dual-enrollment.html)
  * *Pages 8–12*: Documented that dual enrollment students follow distinct pathways and institutional configurations compared to traditional AP coursework.

### Context for Study 1:
Study 1 provides an exact, auditable accounting of the degree to which an AP-only survey indicator omits schools reporting advanced coursework via dual enrollment:
- **Metric A (Conditional Rate among No-AP Schools)**: Among Missouri high schools reporting no AP participation, **92.9%** (105 of 113) report student dual-enrollment participation (sensitivity: 108 of 116, or 93.1%).
- **Metric B (Miss Rate among Schools with Either Route)**: Among schools reporting at least one advanced course route ($N=299$), **35.1%** (105 of 299) report dual enrollment but no AP participation (sensitivity: 108 of 309, or 35.0%).
- **Scope Limit**: The 8 schools (2.6%) reporting neither AP nor dual enrollment are described strictly as reporting neither of those two programs; they are not classified as having "no college-level pathway," as other routes (e.g., International Baccalaureate, career-technical articulation) were not evaluated.

## 2. Umbrella Indicators and Curricular Breadth (The Computer Science Case)

Public school accountability scorecards, state report cards, and commercial aggregator ratings often give schools binary credit for "AP Programs" without examining subject-specific offerings.

### Verified Prior Studies:
* **Code.org Advocacy Coalition, Computer Science Teachers Association (CSTA), & Expanding Computing Education Pathways (ECEP) Alliance (2022)**:
  * *2022 State of Computer Science Education: Understanding Our National Imperative*. [Report Link](https://advocacy.code.org/stateofcs)
  * *State Summaries & Demographics*: Documented state-by-state access disparities in foundational and advanced computer science, showing that AP Computer Science courses (CS A and CS Principles) are concentrated in a small subset of large, well-resourced high schools.
* **College Board (2022)**:
  * *AP Program Participation and Performance Data 2022*. New York, NY: College Board. [Data Archive](https://research.collegeboard.org/programs/ap/data)
  * National disaggregations confirm that STEM courses like AP Computer Science, AP Physics C, and AP Chemistry have substantially lower school-level adoption than humanities courses like AP English Literature or US History.

### Context for Study 2:
Study 2 bounds the epistemic loss of aggregate program reporting:
- Among Missouri public high schools actively reporting AP participation, **65.5%** (127 of 194) reported zero student enrollment in AP Computer Science during 2021–22 (sensitivity: 129 of 201, or 64.2%).
- **Measurement Boundary**: This finding reflects **reported student participation/enrollment** in AP Computer Science (`SCH_APCOMPENR_IND`). It does not establish whether schools offered courses in their catalog that went unenrolled or lacked qualified instructors.

## 3. The Denominator Wedge: Institutional Availability vs. Student Exposure in Physics

The distinction between institutional prevalence and student exposure is a foundational concept in demographic and organizational sociology, but is frequently neglected in policy headlines.

### Verified Prior Studies:
* **White, Susan, & Tesfaye, Casey Langer (American Institute of Physics Statistical Research Center, April 2014)**:
  * *High School Physics Availability: Results from the 2012-13 Nationwide Survey of High School Physics Teachers*. College Park, MD: American Institute of Physics. [Direct PDF](https://aip.brightspotcdn.com/54/24/ca457c052bcff97ac2165b46ec06/hs-physavailability-13.pdf)
  * *Pages 1–3, Table 1 & Figure 1*: Surveyed a nationally representative sample of physics teachers across public and private high schools. White and Tesfaye reported that in SY 2012-13, **91% of high school seniors** attended schools where physics was offered annually, **3%** attended schools where physics was offered in alternating years, and **6%** attended schools where physics was rarely or never offered.
  * *Methodological Note*: The AIP study examined **seniors** across public and private high schools nationally, incorporating alternating-year course cycling. In contrast, Study 3 evaluates all enrolled students (grades 9–12 released enrollment) in Missouri regular public high schools reporting zero physics sections in a single cross-sectional collection (CRDC SY 2021–22). The AIP report provides conceptual precedent for evaluating student exposure alongside school availability, but its specific 6% senior-level figure does not benchmark our 18.20% single-year Missouri grades 9–12 public cohort exposure rate.
* **U.S. Department of Education Office for Civil Rights (OCR, 2016, 2018)**:
  * *2013-14 and 2015-16 Civil Rights Data Collection STEM Summaries*. Washington, DC: U.S. Department of Education.
  * Routine CRDC summaries note that secondary STEM course availability disparities are concentrated in smaller administrative units.

### Context for Study 3:
Study 3 replicates and formalizes this measurement divergence within Missouri's bounded 2021–22 cohort:
- **School Denominator ($P_{\text{school}}$)**: **32.90%** of high schools (101 of 307) report zero physics classes.
- **Student Exposure ($P_{\text{student}}$)**: **18.20%** of students attend those schools (41,616 of 228,637 released enrollment).
- **Denominator Wedge**: **14.70 percentage points** (sensitivity: 15.27 pp).
- **Institutional Scale Driver**: The divergence is directly explained by school size: zero-physics schools average 412 students (median 280), compared to 908 students (median 728) in physics-offering schools.
- **Measurement Interpretation**: Neither metric inherently overstates or biases the other; they describe different populations (administrative units vs. individual learners). The result demonstrates how denominator choice changes the descriptive characterization of course availability.
