# Prior Research Context & Scholarly Positioning

This document situates the three measurement studies within existing empirical literature on advanced secondary coursework, administrative survey indicators, and educational opportunity metrics.

---

## 1. AP vs. Dual-Enrollment: Multiple Pathways to Advanced Credit

A common limitation of early college-readiness research was treating the College Board's Advanced Placement (AP) program as an exhaustive proxy for college-level opportunity.

### Key Prior Studies:
* **Malkus, Nat (AEI, 2016, 2018)**: *The AP Course Pothole: Where High School Size Limits Student Opportunity*. Malkus demonstrated that small high schools face acute structural barriers in offering dedicated AP sections due to minimum class-size thresholds and specialized teacher certification requirements.
* **ACT Research (2017, 2020)**: *Dual Enrollment: Participation and Long-Term Outcomes*. ACT documented that dual enrollment has expanded rapidly across non-suburban and rural communities as a more flexible alternative to AP, often taught in partnership with local community colleges or through online/itinerant arrangements.
* **National Center for Education Statistics (NCES, 2019)**: *Dual Enrollment in High School*. NCES confirmed that nationwide, more than one in three high school graduates took dual credit courses, and in many Midwestern states, dual enrollment participation matches or exceeds AP participation.

### Contribution of Study 1:
Study 1 does not claim to discover dual enrollment. Rather, it provides an exact, auditable accounting of the **blind spot of AP-only indicators**:
- Among Missouri high schools reporting no AP participation, **92.9%** (105 of 113) report dual enrollment.
- Among high schools reporting *either* pathway, an AP-only indicator misses **35.1%** (105 of 299).
- This establishes that evaluating advanced academic opportunity via an AP-only lens systematically misclassifies over a third of active high schools.

## 2. Umbrella Indicators and Curricular Breadth (The Computer Science Case)

Public school accountability scorecards, state report cards, and commercial aggregator ratings often give schools binary credit for "AP Programs" without examining subject-specific offerings.

### Key Prior Studies:
* **Code.org, CSTA, & ECEP Alliance (2021, 2022)**: *State of Computer Science Education*. National reports established that while computer science offerings have grown, AP Computer Science (either CS A or CS Principles) remains heavily concentrated in large suburban high schools.
* **College Board AP Program Results**: Annual disaggregations consistently show that AP STEM courses (outside of Biology and Calculus) have far lower institutional adoption than AP English Literature, US History, or Psychology.

### Contribution of Study 2:
Study 2 bounds the epistemic loss of aggregate program reporting:
- Among Missouri public high schools actively reporting AP participation, **65.5%** (127 of 194) reported zero student enrollment in AP Computer Science during 2021–22.
- An umbrella AP indicator conceals that nearly two-thirds of AP schools have no reported student participation in advanced computer science.

## 3. The Denominator Wedge: Institutional Availability vs. Student Exposure in Physics

The distinction between institutional prevalence and student exposure is a foundational concept in demographic and organizational sociology, but is frequently neglected in policy headlines.

### Key Prior Studies:
* **White, Susan, & Tesfaye, Casey (American Institute of Physics Statistical Research Center, 2014, 2019)**: *High School Physics Availability*. AIP's nationwide teacher surveys documented that while approximately 25%–30% of US public high schools did not offer regular physics classes in a given year, these were disproportionately small schools; consequently, less than 15%–18% of US public high school students attended schools with no physics offerings.
* **Civil Rights Data Collection National Summaries (OCR, 2016, 2018)**: OCR publications routinely highlight both numbers, noting that course availability gaps are heavily concentrated among smaller schools.

### Contribution of Study 3:
Study 3 replicates and formalizes this measurement divergence within Missouri's bounded 2021–22 cohort:
- **School Denominator ($P_{\text{school}}$)**: 32.90% of high schools report zero physics classes.
- **Student Exposure ($P_{\text{student}}$)**: 18.20% of students attend those schools (based on released nonnegative counts).
- **Denominator Wedge**: **14.70 percentage points**.
- The divergence is directly explained by institutional scale: zero-physics schools average 412 students, compared to 908 students in physics-offering schools.
- Neither measure is inherently biased; they describe different populations. Honest descriptive reporting requires presenting both denominators side-by-side.
