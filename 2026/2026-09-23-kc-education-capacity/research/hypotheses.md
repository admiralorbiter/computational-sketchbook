# Research Hypotheses: Kansas City Education Capacity Study

## Core Framing
**Central Research Question:**
*Is insufficient adult instructional capacity the common constraint behind many of the problems teachers experience—and is class size the best observable proxy for that constraint?*

Rather than cataloguing grievances or designing an analysis to validate prior recollections, this project tests competing hypotheses against empirical administrative data. Every hypothesis has defined observation criteria and explicit falsification conditions.

---

### H1a — Ratio / Measurement Illusion Hypothesis
* **Hypothesis:** Reported headline pupil/teacher ratios substantially obscure and understate actual class sizes experienced by students and teachers in standard classrooms.
* **Mechanism:** Staffing ratios divide total enrollment by total certified FTE (including interventionists, reading coaches, speech pathologists, and special education teachers with small caseloads). Consequently, an overall 16:1 ratio can easily coexist with general education core sections of 28–32 students without any deliberate data distortion.
* **Observable Predictions:** 
  1. Median, 75th percentile, and 90th percentile class sizes systematically exceed reported pupil/teacher ratios across districts.
  2. The gap between reported pupil/teacher ratios and actual class sizes is substantial across both elementary and secondary settings.
* **Falsification / Stopping Condition:** If actual section-level distributions track reported staffing ratios closely, H1a is rejected.

---

### H1b — Longitudinal Growth (Ballooning-Class) Hypothesis
* **Hypothesis:** Actual instructional classroom loads have increased over the past 10–15 years, even if reported headline staffing ratios have remained stable.
* **Mechanism:** Shifting resource allocations, expansion of non-classroom specialist positions, or budgetary constraints have expanded core section headcounts over time.
* **Observable Predictions:** 
  1. Section-level panel data demonstrate a secular upward trend in median and upper-quartile class sizes over time.
  2. The percentage of students in high-enrollment sections ($\ge 25, \ge 30, \ge 33$) has risen longitudinally.
* **Falsification / Stopping Condition:** If median and upper-quartile class sizes have been flat or declining over the study period, H1b is rejected. (Note: H1b may be rejected while H1a survives).

---

### H1c — The Allocation Wedge Hypothesis
* **Hypothesis:** A substantial structural wedge exists between reported pupil/teacher ratios and typical general-education classroom section sizes because federal and state teacher FTE counts include instructional personnel who carry small caseloads, provide specialized services, or do not bear standard core classroom rosters.
* **Mechanism & NCES Operational Reality:** NCES explicitly documents that pupil/teacher ratios include teachers for students with disabilities and other specialized teachers, whereas those teachers are generally excluded from class-size calculations. Even under strict federal definitions (instructing students and taking attendance), teacher FTE includes general-ed teachers, SPED caseload teachers, resource teachers, interventionists, co-teachers, pull-out/push-in specialists, alternative program teachers, and low-enrollment specialized electives. Consequently:
  $$\text{Allocation Wedge} = \text{Observable District/School Median Core Section Size} - \text{Reported Pupil/Teacher Ratio}$$
* **Observable Predictions & External Benchmarks:**
  1. **External Benchmark:** State-level NTPS estimates suggest that wedges of several students are plausible and provide an external benchmark (e.g., departmentalized high school estimates: $19.8\text{ KS} / 22.5\text{ MO}$ in 2017–18 and $17.4\text{ KS} / 19.2\text{ MO}$ in 2020–21 vs. $13\text{--}15$ structural ratios). Phase 4 will directly estimate the KC-specific distribution.
  2. **Role Distribution:** Decade-long growth in teacher FTE disproportionately tracks specialized assignments (SPED, intervention, ELL, specialized electives) rather than an expansion of standard general-education sections.
  3. **Roster Loads:** Secondary teacher roster load (total unique students instructed per FTE) remains high (e.g. 130–150 students) despite falling macro staffing ratios.
* **Falsification / Stopping Condition:** If the observable district-level wedge between median core section sizes and staffing ratios is negligible, or if added teacher FTE translated directly into proportional increases in core sections and smaller typical classes, H1c is rejected. (Specific numerical cutoff thresholds deferred until section data distributions are observed).

---

### H2 — Complexity Hypothesis (Heterogeneity and Support Demands)
* **Hypothesis:** Class sizes themselves have remained relatively stable, but the effective burden of a classroom has escalated because student support needs (IEP/SPED status, English language learners, chronic absenteeism, mobility, and academic preparation gaps) have become significantly more heterogeneous and demanding.
* **Mechanism:** Instructional load is a composite of seat count, student heterogeneity, and available adult capacity:
  $$\text{Instructional Load} = \frac{\text{Number of Students} \times \text{Heterogeneity} \times \text{Support Needs} \times \text{Attendance Demands}}{\text{Available Adult Capacity}}$$
* **Observable Predictions:** 
  1. Ratios of classroom teachers to high-need student populations (e.g., SPED caseloads, ELL concentrations) have deteriorated.
  2. Rates of chronic absenteeism, behavioral referrals, or student mobility have increased within typical classrooms even where roster headcount is constant.
* **Falsification / Stopping Condition:** If SPED/ELL proportions and absenteeism rates within general classrooms are stable or accompanied by proportional dedicated adult support, H2 is unsupported as a macro driver.

---

### H3 — Distribution Hypothesis (Averages Concealing the Overcrowded Tail)
* **Hypothesis:** District and school-wide average class sizes appear moderate because low-enrollment specialized sections (small-group intervention, self-contained SPED, specialized electives, AP/IB courses) skew the mean downward, while ordinary core foundation courses (e.g., 9th-grade Algebra I, English I) absorb large, overcrowded rosters.
* **Mechanism:** Uneven staffing allocation concentrates unserved seat volume into mandatory core academic sections.
* **Observable Predictions:** 
  1. **Section-weighted vs. Student-weighted Divergence:** The student-weighted class-size distribution (counting student seat exposures) is substantially right-shifted compared to the section-weighted distribution (counting course sections equally).
  2. **Exposure Thresholds:** A substantial proportion of student instructional hours occur in sections above state and policy benchmarks: $\ge 25$ (MO MSIP 6 recommended for grades 7–12), $\ge 30$, and $\ge 33$ (MO MSIP 6 minimum standard threshold).
* **Falsification / Stopping Condition:** If section-weighted and student-weighted distributions largely coincide and the percentage of student seats in sections $\ge 25$ or $\ge 30$ is negligible, H3 is falsified.

---

### H4 — Null / Counter-Hypothesis
* **Hypothesis:** Neither classroom size nor measurable instructional complexity has deteriorated substantially across the Kansas City region over the last 10–15 years.
* **Mechanism:** Historical recollections of smaller or simpler classrooms represent cognitive availability biases, selective memory, or broader societal stress rather than empirical shifts in school staffing, class rosters, or demographic composition. Alternatively, teacher distress may stem from non-classroom institutional variables (e.g., administrative compliance mandates, curriculum churn, assessment policies) uncaptured by capacity metrics.
* **Role in Research:** Essential epistemic counterweight. If the data demonstrate stable rosters, stable demographic ratios, and moderate distributions across urban, suburban, town, and rural sectors, H4 stands and we abandon claims of ballooning classroom loads.
