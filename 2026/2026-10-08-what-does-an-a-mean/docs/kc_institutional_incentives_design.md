# Kansas City Institutional Incentive Study: Research Design & Policy Framework

## 1. Executive Summary & Epistemological Architecture

This research design governs **Phase 2** of the *What Does an 'A' Mean?* inquiry within the *Computational Sketchbook*.

To maintain methodological rigor, this design establishes a strict separation across three analytical domains:
1. **The Observed Data**: Quantitative measures of high school graduation rates, Missouri MAP Performance Index (MPI) scores, and relative school percentile rankings.
2. **The Institutional Policy Evidence**: Dated primary-source documentation of board policies (e.g., MSBA Policy IKA, IKF), secondary grading manuals, credit-recovery contracts, and administrative guidelines.
3. **The Causal Mechanism**: Empirical hypotheses regarding whether and how institutional rules alter student learning, teacher grading behavior, and credential signaling.

### Descriptive Baseline Established in Phase 1
- **National Trends**: NAEP HSTS (2009–2019) and ACT research panels (2010–2021) document rising high school GPAs alongside stagnant or declining standardized mathematics achievement.
- **Greater Kansas City 2022 Benchmark**: Across 45 comprehensive and charter high schools with complete reporting, four-year graduation rates average 87.2% (median 92.3%) and mathematics MPI scores average 351.0 (median 354.3). The overall correlation across the metropolitan area is positive and fairly strong:
  $$\text{Pearson } r = 0.682, \quad \text{Spearman rank } \rho = 0.667$$
- **School-Level Divergence**: Despite this strong macro-correlation, individual high schools show substantial differences in their relative rankings. The **Graduation–Achievement Rank Difference** ($\Delta_i = \operatorname{PctRank}(G_i) - \operatorname{PctRank}(M_i)$) spans from $-55.6$ to $+63.3$ percentile points ($\text{SD} = 23.8$).

## 2. Institutional Framework & Missouri Statutory Architecture

### 2.1 Accountability Incentives (MSIP 6)
Missouri's School Improvement Program (MSIP 6) Annual Performance Report (APR) assigns substantial weight to high school graduation rates under both Performance (70%) and Continuous Improvement (30%):
- Points are earned for four-year and five-year cohort graduation rates.
- A high school whose students struggle on standardized mathematics exams can secure significant APR points if its cohort graduation rate reaches 90% or higher.

### 2.2 Missouri Statutory and Regulatory Reality
The legal framework governing high school graduation in Missouri combines state minimums with local discretion:
- **State Minimum Graduation Requirements (5 CSR 20-100.230)**: Establishes a statewide baseline of 24 units of credit across designated subject areas (4 ELA, 3 Math, 3 Science, 3 Social Studies, 1 Fine Art, 1 Practical Art, 1 PE, 0.5 Health, 0.5 Personal Finance, and 7 Electives).
- **Local Board Rule-Making Authority (Section 171.011 RSMo)**: Grants local school boards authority to make rules and regulations for district governance. Local boards may establish graduation requirements beyond the state minimum (e.g., 26 or 28 credits) and retain exclusive authority to define local grading scales, credit-earning thresholds, and retake policies.
- **The End-of-Course (EOC) Testing Rule**: Section 160.518 RSMo mandates administering statewide EOC assessments in Algebra I, English II, Biology, and Government. Crucially:
  1. Missouri statute **does not require passing an EOC exam to receive a high school diploma**.
  2. Missouri regulations **do not mandate that EOC scores count for any set percentage of a student's course grade** (unlike North Carolina, which mandated a $\ge 20\%$ weighting; Gershenson, 2018).
  3. Consequently, course letter marks and external exam scale scores operate in separate institutional channels. A student who scores Below Basic on the state Algebra I EOC can receive full course credit toward graduation based on classroom marks.

## 3. The Timing Paradox & Policy Chronologies

A central finding of our primary-source policy audit is that **major district grading reforms in Greater Kansas City were adopted after the 2022 cross-sectional benchmark**:

1. **Kansas City Public Schools (KCPS - 048-078)**:
   - *Policy*: Equitable Grading Policy introducing a 40% minimum grade floor on attempted assignments.
   - *Adoption Date*: Implemented in the **2023–2024 school year** for non-Montessori grades 2–12.
   - *Status in 2022*: Not in effect (traditional 0–100% scale operated in 2021–22).
   - *Revision*: Discontinued/modified following staff feedback after 2023–24.
2. **North Kansas City Schools (NKC 74 - 024-093)**:
   - *Policy*: Transition to Standards-Based Learning (SBL) in secondary grades (4-tier rubric, uncapped reassessments, zero homework penalties).
   - *Adoption Date*: Board approved timeline in January 2024. **Pilot implementation begins in fall 2025** (including North Kansas City High School); full district-wide implementation targeted for **2026–2027**.
   - *Status in 2022*: Not in effect (traditional secondary percentage grading operated in 2021–22).

### Methodological Implication
Because these policies did not exist in 2022, they cannot causally explain the 2022 cross-sectional graduation rates or MPI rankings (e.g., North Kansas City High's +63.3 rank difference in 2022). 

Instead of an invalid retrospective cross-sectional attribution, Phase 2 establishes the baseline and design for **prospective longitudinal case studies**.

## 4. Methodological Distinction: The Graduation–Achievement Rank Difference

The exploratory metric computed across the 45 high schools is defined as:

$$\Delta_i = \operatorname{PctRank}(\text{Grad Rate}_{4\text{yr}, i}) - \operatorname{PctRank}(\text{Math Status MPI}_i)$$

### Interpretive Constraints
1. **Exploratory Screening Tool**: $\Delta_i$ measures relative position within the regional distribution of 45 high schools. It identifies outlier campuses for qualitative and longitudinal inquiry; it is **not** a direct measure of individual credential inflation.
2. **Cohort Non-Identity**: 
   - Four-year graduation rate measures the 12th-grade graduating cohort (Class of 2022).
   - Mathematics MPI measures students tested on high school mathematics EOCs in 2021–22 (predominantly 9th and 10th graders taking Algebra I).
   - These indicators reflect different students tested at different points in their secondary careers.
3. **Aggregate Metric**: School MPI is a continuous weighted average across Below Basic, Basic, Proficient, and Advanced performance levels. It does not represent an individual student proficiency cutoff.

## 5. Focused Longitudinal Research Agenda

Phase 2 narrows the research scope to two tightly dated, high-leverage case studies:

### Case Study A: Kansas City Public Schools (40% Minimum Grading Floor)
- **Policy Intervention**: 40% minimum grade floor on attempted assignments (2023–24).
- **Core Research Questions**:
  1. Did the 40% floor immediately compress course failure rates ('F' grades) in 9th-grade Algebra I and English II?
  2. Did credit accumulation among at-risk 9th and 10th graders increase during 2023–24 relative to pre-policy years (2021–23)?
  3. Did failure rates rebound following policy revisions in 2024–25?
- **Comparison Group**: Within-metro comprehensive high schools in districts maintaining traditional zero-allowed policies (Independence, Lee's Summit).

### Case Study B: North Kansas City Schools (Standards-Based Learning Rollout)
- **Policy Intervention**: SBL pilot in fall 2025 (North Kansas City High), followed by district-wide secondary rollout in 2026–27.
- **Competing Hypotheses**:
  - *H1 (Leniency / Inflation)*: Uncapped reassessments and zero homework penalties make passing easier without improving underlying knowledge.
  - *H2 (Mastery / Achievement Gains)*: Reassessment incentives motivate students to remediate specific learning deficits, leading to genuine gains on standardized EOC exams.
- **Core Research Questions**:
  1. During the 2025–26 pilot, how did course mark distributions in North Kansas City High compare to non-pilot high schools (Oak Park, Staley, Winnetonka)?
  2. Did pilot students demonstrate higher or lower performance on subsequent state EOC exams relative to pre-pilot cohorts?
