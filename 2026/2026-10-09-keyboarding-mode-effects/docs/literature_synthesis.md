# Literature Synthesis: The Mechanics of Mode Effects, Keyboarding, and Construct-Irrelevant Variance

A comprehensive review of empirical literature on digital assessment transitions, keyboarding instruction, and educational measurement.

---

## 1. The Transition to Computer-Based Testing (CBT) and State Mode Penalties

The shift from paper-and-pencil assessments (PBA) to computer-based testing (CBT) across U.S. states began in earnest following the 2010 adoption of Common Core State Standards and the rollout of the PARCC and Smarter Balanced assessment consortia around 2014–2016.

### A. The Massachusetts PARCC Quasi-Experiment (Backes & Cowan, 2019)
- **Citation**: Backes, B., & Cowan, J. (2019). *Is the Pen Mightier Than the Keyboard? The Effect of Online Testing on Measured Student Achievement.* Journal of Policy Analysis and Management, 38(2), 368–397.
- **Context & Design**: During Massachusetts' 2015 and 2016 transition to PARCC testing, districts could choose between administering tests on paper or online. Analyzing over 230,000 students in grades 5–8 using student fixed effects and longitudinal prior achievement controls, Backes and Cowan estimated the causal mode penalty.
- **Empirical Findings**:
  - **Year 1 (2015)**:
    - English Language Arts (ELA): $-0.25$ standard deviations ($\approx -8.4$ scale score points).
    - Mathematics: $-0.10$ standard deviations ($\approx -3.3$ scale score points).
  - **Year 2 (2016)**:
    - ELA penalty attenuated to $-0.13$ SD.
    - Mathematics penalty attenuated to $-0.05$ SD.
- **WWC Evidence Standards**: Reviewed by the federal What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) and determined to meet standards with reservations.
- **Mechanism Analysis**: The authors noted that the penalty was more than twice as large in ELA as in mathematics, pointing to the intensive text transcription and essay composition required by digital ELA exams.

### B. The South Carolina Transition (Egalite & Rapp / Fordham Institute, 2020)
- **Citation**: Egalite, A. J., & Rapp, W. (2020). *Take Note: The Impact of Computer-Based Testing on Student Performance in South Carolina.* Thomas B. Fordham Institute.
- **Findings**:
  - Year 1 ELA penalty: $-0.09$ SD.
  - Year 1 Math penalty: $-0.02$ to $-0.05$ SD.
  - **Equity Gradient**: Economically disadvantaged students experienced significantly greater score depression ($-0.12$ SD in ELA) compared to non-disadvantaged peers ($-0.06$ SD), establishing that mode penalties interact directly with student socioeconomic status and home technology access.

## 2. Federal Experience: The 2017 NAEP Digitally Based Assessment (DBA) Transition

The National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), known as "The Nation's Report Card," transitioned its core fourth- and eighth-grade reading and mathematics assessments to digital devices in 2017.

### A. Item-Type Format Wedges (NCES 2017 Transition Whitepaper)
The NCES Mode Evaluation Study (*2017 NAEP Transition to Digitally Based Assessments in Mathematics and Reading at Grades 4 and 8: Mode Evaluation Study*) revealed a dramatic divergence based on question format:
- **Selected-Response (Multiple-Choice) Items**:
  - Grade 4 Reading: $-0.01$ SD (statistically indistinguishable from 0).
  - Grade 8 Reading: $+0.01$ SD (negligible).
- **Constructed-Response (Typing / Equation Entry) Items**:
  - Grade 4 Reading: **$-0.18$ SD** (substantial and statistically significant penalty, $p < 0.001$).
  - Grade 8 Reading: **$-0.08$ SD** (moderate penalty, less than half of Grade 4).
- **Substantive Conclusion**: The digital testing penalty at Grade 4 is almost entirely concentrated on items that require typing! When fourth-grade students only need to click an answer, their digital score matches their paper score. When they must construct an answer using a keyboard, they suffer a near-fifth of a standard deviation penalty.

### B. The 2017 NAEP Writing Assessment Collapse
Even more dramatically, the 2017 NAEP Grade 4 and Grade 8 Writing assessment could not be reported publicly:
- **Official Status**: Suppressed / Unreportable by the National Assessment Governing Board (NAGB).
- **Underlying Causes**:
  1. **Typing Speed Confounding**: Average student typing speed was insufficient to generate the essay lengths achieved in paper pilot administrations.
  2. **Device Disparities**: Substantial performance differences emerged between students tested on laptops with full physical keyboards versus tablets with touchscreen virtual keyboards or clip-on keyboards.
  3. **Response Length Compression**: Word counts dropped by 30–40% on digital entry for young students, which severely distorted the automated and human holistic rubric scoring.

## 3. The Keyboarding Mechanism: Is Typing Instruction the Solution?

While the digital assessment penalty is real, the proposition that simply teaching keyboarding classes will eliminate this penalty or raise national test scores encounters severe empirical hurdles.

### A. The Tennessee Middle School Keyboarding Study (NBEA)
- **Design**: Quasi-experimental comparison in grades 6–8 comparing students who completed a 9-week standalone keyboarding course against students who did not, evaluating their performance on computerized writing assessments.
- **Finding**: Effect size of $+0.04$ SD ($p = 0.48$), statistically indistinguishable from zero.
- **Interpretation**: Simply teaching isolated keystroke drills or touch typing in a nine-week silo does not automatically improve complex essay composition or reading comprehension under timed conditions.

### B. Cognitive Load and the "Simple View of Writing"
Psycholinguistic research (Berninger & Amtmann, 1999; Graham, 1990; McCutchen, 1996) provides a rigorous explanation:
1. **Transcription Fluency**: Until typing reaches automaticity (roughly $\approx 25$ WPM), the physical act of typing consumes significant executive working memory.
2. **Cognitive Competition**: When transcription is non-automatic, working memory is drained away from planning, vocabulary selection, syntactic framing, and self-monitoring.
3. **Threshold Mechanics**: Keyboarding instruction is a **necessary enabling threshold**, not an independent driver of writing quality. Once transcription is automatic, student performance is governed by substantive academic knowledge.

## 4. Digital Literacy Beyond Keystrokes: The ICILS Evidence

The decline in students' digital competence is broader than physical typing:
- **IEA ICILS (International Computer and Information Literacy Study)**:
  - U.S. 8th graders scored **519** in 2018.
  - U.S. 8th graders dropped to **482** in 2023 ($-37$ points, $-0.37$ SD, $p < 0.001$).
  - Over 43% of U.S. 8th graders scored at Level 1 or below (basic deficiency in evaluating digital information, structuring files, or managing computer applications).
  - The socioeconomic gap was 63 points between high-SES and low-SES families.
- **Key Insight**: Students today are "touchscreen natives," comfortable with passive video streaming, smartphone scrolling, and touch apps, but frequently lack proficiency with desktop operating systems, file directories, physical keyboards, and word processors.
